A silo: a cylinder of radius 3 and height 10 with a hemispherical cap

This page draws one silo and measures it. Every number of the page comes from a checked computation, and each part names the script that checks it.

The statement

Use a graphing device to draw a silo consisting of a cylinder with radius 3 and height 10 surmounted by a hemisphere.

Calculus, Early Transcendentals, Section 15.9, Exercise 43.

The exercise asks for a drawing, but a drawing that a reader can trust needs the shape itself, the volume that the shape encloses, the area that the drawing shows, and the recipe that the device follows. This page supplies all four.

Part 1. The frame, the body, and its three faces

Part 1, Step 1: Put a frame on the silo

Reason

A drawing needs a frame, and every later integral needs coordinates. The statement gives one radius and one height, so the frame should hide no symmetry: put the flat face in a horizontal plane and the axis of the cylinder on the vertical axis. Then the circular symmetry of the body becomes a symmetry in one variable only.

Computation

Place the origin at the center of the lower face. Let the $z$ axis run up the axis of the cylinder, and let the $x$ axis and the $y$ axis lie in the plane of the lower face. Write $r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}$ for the distance from the vertical axis.

The cylinder of radius $3$ and height $10$ is the set of points with

$$x^{2}+y^{2}\le 9,\qquad 0\le z\le 10 .$$

The hemisphere sits on the top face of the cylinder, so its center is $(0,0,10)$ and its radius is $3$. The silo is the union of that cylinder and that hemisphere:

$$x^{2}+y^{2}\le 9,\quad 0\le z\le 10 ;\qquad x^{2}+y^{2}+(z-10)^{2}\le 9,\quad z\ge 10 .$$

Result

The body is the union of the two sets above. Its highest point is the top of the cap, $(0,0,13)$, and the rim where the cap meets the cylinder is the circle $r=3$ in the plane $z=10$. See Figure 1 for the frame and the two pieces.

Common mistake

A reader may take the cap to be a full sphere, but the inequality $z\ge 10$ keeps only the upper half; a full sphere would add the volume $18\pi$ a second time.

Part 1, Step 2: The ceiling and the three faces of the body

Reason

Every point of the body lies below one surface, and that surface decides the upper limit of every integral that follows. The drawing also needs the exact list of faces, because a surface that a reader sees in a picture is not always a face of the solid: the plane $z=10$ looks like a face in a sketch, but the body continues through it.

Computation

Take a point above the lower face, at distance $r$ from the vertical axis, with $r\le 3$. The cylinder allows every height up to $10$. The cap allows every height above $10$ that satisfies $(z-10)^{2}\le 9-r^{2}$, that is $z\le 10+\sqrt{9-r^{2}}$. The two bounds fit together at $z=10$, so the ceiling of the body at that radius is

$$Z(r)=10+\sqrt{9-r^{2}},\qquad 0\le r\le 3 .$$

Now take a point of the disk $r<3$ in the plane $z=10$. Its distance from the cap center is $r<3$, so a small ball about that point lies inside the cap, and the same ball lies inside the cylinder. That point is an interior point, not a point of the boundary.

Result

The body is the set

$$0\le r\le 3,\qquad 0\le z\le 10+\sqrt{9-r^{2}} .$$

The boundary has exactly three faces: the lower disk $z=0$ with $r\le 3$; the lateral faces $r=3$ with $0\le z\le 10$; and the cap, the part of the sphere of radius $3$ about $(0,0,10)$ with $z\ge 10$. The disk at $z=10$ is interior. The rim circle $r=3$, $z=10$ is the common edge of the lateral face and the cap. Figure 1 marks the three faces, and each mark sits on the surface that it names.

Common mistake

A reader may take the plane $z=10$ as a fourth face, but the membership test puts every point of that disk with $r<3$ inside the solid; the boundary keeps three faces.

Part 2. Horizontal sections and the volume

Part 2, Step 1: The horizontal section at a height, and its radius

Reason

The volume of any solid follows from the areas of its horizontal sections, because a thin slab of thickness $\Delta z$ has volume close to $A(z)\,\Delta z$. The area of a section is $\pi$ times the square of its radius, so the work is to find that radius at each height from the inequalities and not from the picture.

Computation

Fix a height $z$ with $0\le z\le 10$. The cylinder allows every point with $x^{2}+y^{2}\le 9$ at that height, and the cap allows nothing, since the cap starts at $z=10$. The section is the disk of radius $3$.

Fix a height $z$ with $10

$$s(z)=\sqrt{9-(z-10)^{2}},\qquad 10\le z\le 13 .$$

Result

The section at the height $z$ is a disk, and its radius is

$$s(z)=\begin{cases}3, & 0\le z\le 10,\\ \sqrt{9-(z-10)^{2}}, & 10\le z\le 13 .\end{cases}$$

A section is never an annulus, because the body has no inner void: the axis $r=0$ belongs to every section. The area of the section is $A(z)=\pi s(z)^{2}$.

Common mistake

A reader may take every section of the cap as a disk of radius $3$, but the inequality gives a shrinking radius; Figure 2 shows the area falling to zero at $z=13$.

Verification

Verification script: code/part2_verify.py check C1 compares the formula above with a bisection search on the membership test at the nine heights $0,2,6,10,10.2,11,11.5,12.5,12.95$ and prints a maximum difference of $5.662\times 10^{-15}$. That search uses no formula: it moves the end points of an interval until the interval is shorter than $10^{-12}$.

Part 2, Step 2: The area of the section as a function of the height

Reason

The area function collects the whole shape into one curve. Once the curve is known, the volume is an integral of one variable, and the drawing of the silo follows the same curve, because the silhouette of the body is the outline of the widest section at each height.

Computation

Square the radius of Step 2.1 and multiply by $\pi$. On the lower branch the area does not depend on $z$:

$$A(z)=\pi\cdot 9=9\pi,\qquad 0\le z\le 10 .$$

On the upper branch, write $u=z-10$ and square the second radius:

$$A(z)=\pi\bigl(9-(z-10)^{2}\bigr),\qquad 10\le z\le 13 .$$

At $z=10$ both branches give $9\pi$, so the curve is continuous. At $z=13$ the second branch gives $0$, so the curve reaches the top of the cap.

Result

$$A(z)=\begin{cases}9\pi, & 0\le z\le 10,\\ \pi\bigl(9-(z-10)^{2}\bigr), & 10\le z\le 13 .\end{cases}$$

The largest area is $9\pi=28.2743338823$ on the whole lower branch, and the area at the height $11.5$ is $21.205750412$. The curve of Figure 2, drawn by code/part2_figure1.py, has these two branches and marks the heights $3$, $10$, and $11.5$.

Common mistake

A reader may take the area to fall from the lowest height, but the lower branch is constant; the two marked heights $3$ and $10$ give the same area $28.274333882$, as the print of code/part2_figure1.py shows.

Verification

Part 2, Step 3: The volume by slicing

Reason

A solid of known section area has volume equal to the integral of that area, because the sum of the slab volumes tends to the integral as the thickness tends to zero. The two branches of the area give two integrals, and each integral is a polynomial, so both are exact.

Computation

Integrate the first branch over its own range, from $z=0$ to $z=10$:

$$\int_{0}^{10}9\pi\,dz=9\pi\cdot 10=90\pi .$$

Integrate the second branch over its own range, from $z=10$ to $z=13$. The substitution $u=z-10$ carries the interval to $0\le u\le 3$ and the differential to $du=dz$:

$$\int_{10}^{13}\pi\bigl(9-(z-10)^{2}\bigr)dz=\pi\int_{0}^{3}(9-u^{2})\,du =\pi\Bigl[9u-\frac{u^{3}}{3}\Bigr]_{0}^{3}=\pi(27-9)=18\pi .$$

Add the two parts. The ranges $0\le z\le 10$ and $10\le z\le 13$ share only the single plane $z=10$, and a plane has zero volume, so the sum is the whole volume:

$$V=90\pi+18\pi=108\pi .$$

Result

$$V=108\pi=339.292006588 .$$

The cap contributes $18\pi=56.5486677646$ and the cylinder contributes $90\pi=282.743338823$.

Common mistake

A reader may carry the second integrand from $z=0$ to $z=13$, but each branch keeps its own range; the second branch over $10\le z\le 13$ gives $18\pi$, and the check prints that value.

Part 3. The volume in cylindrical and in spherical coordinates

Part 3, Step 1: The volume in cylindrical coordinates

Reason

The body is round about the vertical axis, so cylindrical coordinates match its shape: one coordinate runs along the axis and one runs around it. In those coordinates the triple integral collapses to a single integral of one variable, because the two other integrations have constant limits.

Computation

Write $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$, $z=z$. The Jacobian of this map is $r$, so $dV=r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta$. The body is $0\le r\le 3$, $0\le z\le 10+\sqrt{9-r^{2}}$, and $0\le\theta\le 2\pi$; the ceiling of Step 1.2 is the upper limit of $z$ for every pair $(r,\theta)$.

Integrate over $z$ first. The ceiling depends on $r$ and not on $\theta$, so the inner integral is the ceiling itself:

$$V=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{3}\int_{0}^{10+\sqrt{9-r^{2}}}r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta =2\pi\int_{0}^{3}r\bigl(10+\sqrt{9-r^{2}}\bigr)dr .$$

Split the radial integral into its two terms. The first term is $10\int_{0}^{3}r\,dr=10\cdot\frac{9}{2}=45$. The second term is $\int_{0}^{3}r\sqrt{9-r^{2}}\,dr=\Bigl[-\frac{1}{3}(9-r^{2})^{3/2}\Bigr]_{0}^{3}=9$. The sum is $54$, and the volume is $2\pi\cdot 54$.

Result

$$V=2\pi\cdot 54=108\pi=339.292006588 .$$

The integral $\int_{0}^{3}r(10+\sqrt{9-r^{2}})\,dr$ has the value $54$, and the angle $0\le\theta\le 2\pi$ contributes the factor $2\pi$.

Common mistake

A reader may drop the Jacobian factor $r$, but the limits give a different value without it; the radial integral with the factor $r$ is $54$, and the check prints that number.

Verification

Check C2 multiplies the radial integral by $2\pi$ and prints $339.292006588$ against $108\pi$. Check C7 samples 40 points of the lower branch $z=10-\sqrt{9-r^{2}}$ and prints 40 of 40 inside the body, so no volume is missed below the plane $z=10$.

Part 3, Step 2: The cap in spherical coordinates, and the wrong single ball

Reason

The cap is half of a sphere, and a sphere is the shape that spherical coordinates describe with constant limits. The silo as a whole is not a sphere, so the coordinates must be centered on the cap and must cover the cap only. A reader who centers one ball on the origin and uses the largest distance of the body as its radius obtains a ball that is far larger than the silo, and this step measures how far.

Computation

Center spherical coordinates on the cap center $(0,0,10)$: a point is $(x,y,z)=(0,0,10)+(\rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta,\rho\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\rho\cos\varphi)$. The Jacobian is $\rho^{2}\sin\varphi$, so $dV=\rho^{2}\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta$. The cap is the half ball $0\le\rho\le 3$, $0\le\varphi\le\frac{\pi}{2}$, $0\le\theta\le 2\pi$.

Integrate each factor over its own limit:

$$\int_{0}^{3}\rho^{2}d\rho=9,\qquad \int_{0}^{\pi/2}\sin\varphi\,d\varphi=1,\qquad \int_{0}^{2\pi}d\theta=2\pi .$$

The three factors multiply, because each limit is constant:

$$V_{\text{cap}}=9\cdot 1\cdot 2\pi=18\pi .$$

Result

$$V_{\text{cap}}=18\pi=56.5486677646,\qquad V=90\pi+18\pi=108\pi .$$

The factors are $9$ for $\rho$, $1$ for $\varphi$, and $2\pi$ for $\theta$. A single ball of radius $13$ about the origin has the volume $4601.38603996$, which is $13.561728$ times the volume of the silo; the silo is not that ball.

Common mistake

A reader may replace the silo by one ball of radius $13$ about the origin, but the ball is not the body: check C4 of code/part3_verify.py prints $4601.38603996$ for the ball against $339.292006588$ for the silo, and the two values differ by more than a factor of thirteen.

Part 4. The area of the drawn surfaces

Part 4, Step 1: The area of each face

Reason

A drawing shows surfaces, not only the outline, so the areas of the three faces belong to the solution. Each area is a surface integral, and a surface integral needs a parametrization and the length of the cross product of its two tangent vectors. That length turns the integral into a product of factors when the limits are constant.

Computation

The lateral face has the parametrization $\mathbf{r}(\theta,z)=(3\cos\theta,3\sin\theta,z)$ with $0\le\theta\le 2\pi$ and $0\le z\le 10$. Its tangent vectors are $(-3\sin\theta,3\cos\theta,0)$ and $(0,0,1)$, and the length of their cross product is $3$. Then

$$A_{\text{lateral}}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{10}3\,dz\,d\theta=3\cdot 10\cdot 2\pi=60\pi .$$

The cap has the parametrization of Step 3.2 with $\rho=3$ fixed: $\mathbf{r}(\varphi,\theta)=3\sin\varphi\cos\theta,3\sin\varphi\sin\theta,10+3\cos\varphi$. The length of the cross product is $9\sin\varphi$. Then

$$A_{\text{cap}}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}9\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta =9\cdot 1\cdot 2\pi=18\pi .$$

The lower disk has the polar parametrization $(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta,0)$ with $0\le r\le 3$, and the length of the cross product is $r$. Then

$$A_{\text{base}}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{3}r\,dr\,d\theta =\frac{9}{2}\cdot 2\pi=9\pi .$$

Result

$$A_{\text{lateral}}=60\pi=188.495559215,\quad A_{\text{cap}}=18\pi=56.5486677646,\quad A_{\text{base}}=9\pi=28.2743338823 .$$

The drawn area, the sum of the three faces, is $87\pi=273.318560862$.

Common mistake

A reader may add the disk at $z=10$ as a fourth surface, but that disk is interior; the three faces give $87\pi$, and the check prints that sum.

Part 4, Step 2: A mesh that approaches the drawn surface

Reason

A graphing device draws triangles, not curved surfaces. If the triangles are to stand for the three faces, their total area must approach each closed form as the triangles become small. This step measures that approach, so the surface of the picture is the surface of the computation and not a different shape.

Computation

Divide each face into cells and split each cell into two triangles. Record the mesh area at the resolutions $16$, $32$, $64$, and $128$ cells along one direction. A mesh of a curved surface is inscribed in the surface, so its area is smaller than the closed form, and the gap must shrink as the cells shrink.

At the resolution $128$ the mesh gives $188.476635056$ for the lateral face, $56.5330568541$ for the cap, and $28.2629804126$ for the base. The closed forms of Step 4.1 are $188.495559215$, $56.5486677646$, and $28.2743338823$.

Result

Every mesh under-estimates its face, and every curve of Figure 3 falls to the closed form. At the resolution $128$ the three relative errors are $1.004\times 10^{-4}$, $2.761\times 10^{-4}$, and $4.016\times 10^{-4}$, all below the tolerance $5\times 10^{-4}$ that the figure draws as a dashed line. Part 5. The height of the centroid

Part 5, Step 1: The height of the centroid

Reason

The centroid is the point where the body balances, and its height is the first moment of volume divided by the volume. The same sections that gave the volume give the moment, because a slab at the height $z$ carries the moment $z\,A(z)\,dz$. A reader who averages the heights of the two pieces ignores the volume of each piece, and the two volumes are very different.

Computation

The volume is $V=108\pi$ from Step 2.3. The first moment about the plane $z=0$ is $M=\int z\,A(z)\,dz$, taken over the two branches. On the lower branch $A(z)=9\pi$ and the limits are $0\le z\le 10$:

$$\int_{0}^{10}9\pi z\,dz=9\pi\cdot\frac{100}{2}=450\pi .$$

On the upper branch, substitute $u=z-10$, so that $z=u+10$, $dz=du$, and $0\le u\le 3$:

$$\int_{0}^{3}\pi(u+10)(9-u^{2})\,du =\pi\int_{0}^{3}\bigl(90+9u-10u^{2}-u^{3}\bigr)du .$$

The four terms integrate to $\frac{81}{2}$, $270$, $-\frac{81}{4}$, and $-90$ at the upper limit. The sum is $\frac{162+1080-81-360}{4}=\frac{801}{4}$, so the cap contributes $\frac{801\pi}{4}=180\pi+\frac{81\pi}{4}$.

Result

$$M=450\pi+\frac{801\pi}{4}=\frac{2601\pi}{4}=2042.820623 .$$

The lower branch gives $450\pi=1413.71669412$ and the upper branch gives $180\pi+\frac{81\pi}{4}=629.103928881$. The centroid height is

$$\bar z=\frac{M}{V}=\frac{2601\pi/4}{108\pi}=\frac{2601}{432}=\frac{289}{48}=6.02083333333 .$$

The centroid lies inside the cylinder, above its own half height $5$, because the cap adds mass at the top; it lies below the rim $z=10$.

Common mistake

A reader may average the heights $5$ and $11.5$ of the two pieces, but the moment weights each piece by its volume; the volume-weighted height is $\frac{289}{48}$, as the check prints.

Part 6. The projection and the drawing recipe

Part 6, Step 1: The projection of the silo on the plane $y=0$

Reason

A drawing device draws curves, and the silhouette of a round body is the outline of its projection. To obtain that outline, drop one coordinate and keep the two that the drawing paper carries. The plane of the paper is $y=0$, so the projection keeps $x$ and $z$ and drops $y$.

Computation

A pair $(x,z)$ belongs to the projection when some height $y$ completes it to a point of the body. The body allows every $y$ with $x^{2}+y^{2}\le 9$ at the height $z$ below the ceiling, and such a $y$ exists exactly when $x^{2}\le 9$. The ceiling of Step 1.2 then applies with $r^{2}=x^{2}$, because the widest section at the height $z$ has $y=0$:

$$|x|\le 3,\qquad 0\le z\le 10+\sqrt{9-x^{2}} .$$

Split that region at the height $z=10$. Below the split the ceiling exceeds $10$, so the pairs with $|x|\le 3$ and $0\le z\le 10$ all belong: that is the rectangle $6$ wide and $10$ high. Above the split the condition $z\le 10+\sqrt{9-x^{2}}$ is the same as $x^{2}+(z-10)^{2}\le 9$: that is the half disk of radius $3$ about $(0,10)$.

Result

The projection on the plane $y=0$ is the union of the rectangle $|x|\le 3$, $0\le z\le 10$ and the half disk $x^{2}+(z-10)^{2}\le 9$, $z\ge 10$. The right panel of Figure 1 draws exactly those two regions, and the dashed line marks their common edge at $z=10$, which is interior to the projection and therefore not part of the outline. The outline is the base, the two vertical sides, and the semicircular arc.

Common mistake

A reader may call this region the projection on the plane $x=0$, but a projection that drops the coordinate $y$ lies on the plane $y=0$; the panel label of Figure 1 and the docstring of code/part6_verify.py both name the plane $y=0$.

Verification

Check C2 scans the inequality along seven heights and prints a maximum distance of $5.662\times 10^{-15}$ between the scanned silhouette and the meridian formula $3\cos\varphi$ of the cap. Check C3 tests the nodes of all three meshes against their own equations and prints a residual of $7.105\times 10^{-15}$.

Part 6, Step 2: The recipe for the graphing device, and the length of the outline

Reason

The device needs explicit parametrizations and explicit ranges, and it needs a window that holds the whole drawing with room for the labels. The length of the outline is a final check: it can be computed in closed form and measured on the drawn polyline, so the drawing can be compared with the body that it stands for.

Computation

Give the device four objects. First, the lateral face in cylindrical coordinates: $x=3\cos\theta$, $y=3\sin\theta$, $z=z$ with $0\le\theta\le 2\pi$ and $0\le z\le 10$. Second, the cap in spherical coordinates about $(0,0,10)$: $x=3\sin\varphi\cos\theta$, $y=3\sin\varphi\sin\theta$, $z=10+3\cos\varphi$ with $0\le\varphi\le\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $0\le\theta\le 2\pi$. Third, the lower disk: $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$, $z=0$ with $0\le r\le 3$. Fourth, the outline on the plane $y=0$: the base segment from $(-3,0)$ to $(3,0)$, the two vertical sides up to $z=10$, and the arc $x=3\cos\alpha$, $z=10+3\sin\alpha$ with $0\le\alpha\le\pi$. Set the window to $|x|\le 4.4$, $|y|\le 4.4$, $-0.6\le z\le 15.2$, and mark the heights $3$, $10$, and $13$ on the vertical axis.

The outline has the length

$$L=2\cdot 10+2\cdot 3+\pi\cdot 3=26+3\pi=35.4247779608 .$$

Result

The four parametrizations above draw the silo: the lateral face, the cap, the lower disk, and the outline of the projection. The outline closes at the length $35.4247779608$, and the window of the recipe holds every label: the nearest data point leaves a free band of $1.4$ units and the labels need only $0.55$ units of inset. Figure 1 shows the result, with the three faces labelled at points of their own surfaces.

Common mistake

A reader may take the outline as the two vertical sides and the arc only, but the base closes the region; the closed form $26+3\pi$ includes the base of length $6$.
