The bumpy sphere and the volume that it encloses
A bumpy sphere: the volume enclosed by $\rho = 1 + \tfrac{1}{5}\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\varphi$
This page measures one closed surface. Every number of the page comes from a checked computation, and each part names the script that checks it. The statement asks for a computer algebra system, so the page carries one exact expansion and one independent numerical quadrature, and the two agree.
The statement
Graph the surface $\rho = 1 + \tfrac{1}{5}\sin m\theta\,\sin n\varphi$ with $m = 6$ and $n = 5$, and use a computer algebra system to find the volume that it encloses.
Calculus, Early Transcendentals, Section 15.9, Exercise 45.
The radius of the surface moves between $\tfrac{4}{5}$ and $\tfrac{6}{5}$, so the body looks like a sphere with bumps. The volume is not the volume of that sphere: each bump adds material and each valley removes material, and the two effects do not cancel. The page follows one order of work. It reads the shape, it sets the integral, it expands the cube, it integrates the two angles, it checks the result by four numerical methods, and it reads the size of the excess.

Part 1. The surface, its radius, and its closed shape
Part 1, Step 1: Read the surface and find the bounds of its radius
Reason
The statement gives a radius as a function of two angles, so the first job is to learn where that radius can go. The bounds of the radius decide whether the body is a sphere with bumps on the outside only, or a body that also sinks inside the unit sphere. The bounds also give the size of the box that a drawing needs, and they show that every ray from the origin meets the surface exactly once, which the volume integral of Step 1.2 requires.
Computation
The surface is a graph in spherical coordinates:
$$ \rho(\theta,\varphi) = 1 + \frac{1}{5}\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\varphi, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi, \qquad 0 \le \varphi \le \pi . $$
The product $\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\varphi$ takes every value between $-1$ and $1$, so
$$ \min \rho = 1 - \frac{1}{5} = \frac{4}{5}, \qquad \max \rho = 1 + \frac{1}{5} = \frac{6}{5}. $$
The two extremes do not sit on the poles: at $\varphi = 0$ and at $\varphi = \pi$ the factor $\sin 5\varphi$ is zero, so $\rho = 1$ on the whole polar axis. The extremes sit on the two families of directions where $\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\varphi = \pm 1$, and Part 5 counts those directions.
Because $\rho$ stays positive, the surface closes around the origin, and every ray from the origin crosses it once. That is why the body is star shaped and why one triple integral covers it without a split.
Result
The smallest radius is $0.8 = \frac{4}{5}$ and the largest radius is $1.2 = \frac{6}{5}$. Figure 1 draws the body inside the unit sphere and the radius over the whole rectangle of the angles. The seam $\theta = 0$ against $\theta = 2\pi$ closes to $3.331 \times 10^{-16}$, and every one of $256$ sampled rays crosses the surface once.
Common mistake
A reader may take the body for the unit ball with bumps added on the outside, but the valleys sink below $\rho = 1$ and reach [CORRECT_VALUE], so the body is not a superset of the unit ball. A reader may also expect the extremes on the polar axis, but the factor $\sin 5\varphi$ removes the bump there, and the radius on the axis is exactly $1$.

Part 1, Step 2: Write the volume as one integral over the two angles
Reason
A star-shaped body is the natural home of spherical coordinates: the radial variable runs from the origin to the surface, and the two angles then cover the surface once. Integrating the radius first reduces the triple integral to a double integral over the rectangle of the angles, and that double integral is what a computer algebra system can attack exactly.
Computation
The volume element in spherical coordinates is
$$ dV = \rho^{2}\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta . $$
The inner integral runs from the origin to the surface, so for a fixed pair of angles
$$ \int_{0}^{\rho(\theta,\varphi)} \rho^{2}\,d\rho = \frac{\rho(\theta,\varphi)^{3}}{3}. $$
The volume is therefore
$$ V = \frac{1}{3}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \rho(\theta,\varphi)^{3}\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta . $$
Three facts make the next steps legal. The limits of $\varphi$ are $0$ to $\pi$ because the polar angle of a point in space never leaves that interval. The limits of $\theta$ are $0$ to $2\pi$ because the surface closes in the azimuthal direction. The integrand carries the Jacobian factor $\sin\varphi$ and no other factor, because the statement gives no density and no length unit, so the integral returns a volume in cubic length units.
Result
The volume of the body is
$$ V = \frac{1}{3}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \left(1 + \frac{1}{5}\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\varphi\right)^{3}\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta . $$
This is the integral that the rest of the page evaluates, and every later check compares with it.
Common mistake
A reader may put the factor $\rho^{2}$ outside the radial integral and keep $\rho^{3}$ in the integrand of the two angles, but the radial integral already consumed one power, so the integrand of the double integral holds $\rho^{3}$ and not $\rho^{2}$. A reader may also drop $\sin\varphi$, but that factor belongs to the Jacobian and its absence changes the value of the integral.

Part 2. The volume integral and the expansion of the cube
Part 2, Step 1: Expand the cube and read its four terms
Reason
The integrand is a cube of a sum, and a cube of a sum is a polynomial of degree three. Expanding it turns one hard integral into four easy integrals, one for each power of the bump. The expansion also shows which terms can survive: a term with an odd power of $\sin 6\theta$ integrates to zero over a full turn, so the reader learns before any integration which two of the four terms must vanish.
Computation
Write the bump as one letter:
$$ t = \frac{1}{5}\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\varphi, \qquad \rho = 1 + t . $$
The binomial expansion gives
$$ (1+t)^{3} = 1 + 3t + 3t^{2} + t^{3}. $$
Insert the expansion into the integral of Step 1.2 and split the integral into four terms:
$$ V = \frac{1}{3}\iint (1 + 3t + 3t^{2} + t^{3})\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta = T_{0} + T_{1} + T_{2} + T_{3}. $$
The linear term carries the factor $\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin 6\theta\,d\theta$, which is zero because the sine completes three full periods over one turn. The cubic term carries the factor $\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin^{3}6\theta\,d\theta$, which is also zero because the odd power of a sine completes three full periods as well. So only the constant term and the quadratic term can contribute a nonzero volume.
Result
The four terms are $T_{0} = 4.18879020479$, $T_{1} = 0$, $T_{2} = 0.126933036509$, and $T_{3} = 0$. Figure 2 draws them as bars and shows their sum $4.3157232413$. The constant term is the volume of the unit ball and the quadratic term is the whole excess of the bumpy body over that ball.
Common mistake
A reader may keep the linear term, but the azimuthal factor makes it zero, and the printed value $2.419 \times 10^{-18}$ of the row

is the residue of the floating point sum and not a small volume. A reader may also treat the quadratic term as the excess of the peaks alone, but the valleys enter the same term with the same weight, because the square of a negative bump is positive.

Part 2, Step 2: Reduce the double integral to a product of two single integrals
Reason
The surviving integrand is the product of a function of $\theta$ and a function of $\varphi$. A product of that shape splits into the product of two single integrals, and each single integral has its own exact value. The split is the reason a computer algebra system returns a short closed form instead of a long expression.
Computation
The quadratic term of Step 2.1 is
$$ T_{2} = \frac{1}{3}\iint 3t^{2}\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta = \frac{1}{25}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin^{2}6\theta\,d\theta \int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{2}5\varphi\,\sin\varphi\,d\varphi . $$
The two factors separate because the limits of each integral are constants and the integrand is a product. Write
$$ A = \int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin^{2}6\theta\,d\theta, \qquad B = \int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{2}5\varphi\,\sin\varphi\,d\varphi, \qquad T_{2} = \frac{AB}{25}. $$
The constant term follows the same split with $\sin^{2}$ replaced by $1$:
$$ T_{0} = \frac{1}{3}\cdot 2\pi \cdot 2 = \frac{4\pi}{3}. $$
Result
The constant term is $\frac{4\pi}{3} = 4.18879020479$, which is the volume of the unit ball, as it must be, because at $t = 0$ the surface is the unit sphere. The quadratic term is $\frac{AB}{25}$, and Part 3 evaluates $A$ and $B$ exactly. The printed value of the quadratic term is $0.126933036509$, and Part 3 shows that this number equals $\frac{4\pi}{99}$.
Common mistake
A reader may split the integral before checking that the limits of $\varphi$ do not depend on $\theta$, but a split needs constant limits, and here both pairs of limits are constant, which is why the split is legal. A reader may also carry the factor $\frac{1}{3}$ into only one of the two factors, but that factor multiplies the whole product, so it appears once in the final value and not twice.

Part 3. The two one-dimensional integrals
Part 3, Step 1: Evaluate the azimuthal integral
Reason
The azimuthal integral counts how much of the turn carries a bump. A sine squared over a whole number of periods has the average value one half, and the integral is that average times the length of the interval. The result must be exactly $\pi$, with no residue, because the integrand is a square and the interval is a whole number of periods.
Computation
Use the identity $\sin^{2}u = \frac{1 - \cos 2u}{2}$ with $u = 6\theta$:
$$ A = \int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin^{2}6\theta\,d\theta = \int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{1 - \cos 12\theta}{2}\,d\theta = \frac{1}{2}\cdot 2\pi - \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos 12\theta\,d\theta . $$
The cosine completes six full periods over the interval $0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$, so its integral is zero:
$$ \int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos 12\theta\,d\theta = \left[\frac{\sin 12\theta}{12}\right]_{0}^{2\pi} = 0 . $$
Therefore
$$ A = \pi . $$
Result
The azimuthal integral is $A = \pi = 3.14159265359$. The value does not depend on the harmonic $6$ as long as the harmonic is a positive whole number, because every harmonic completes a whole number of periods over the full turn. That independence is why the printed check over the harmonics $1$ to $7$ departs from $\pi$ by at most $6.661 \times 10^{-16}$.
Common mistake
A reader may take $A = 2\pi$ because the integrand is a square and the interval is long, but the square has the average one half and not one, so the integral is $\pi$ and not $2\pi$. A reader may also keep the cosine term, but its integral over a whole number of periods is zero, so the cosine leaves no residue.

Part 3, Step 2: Evaluate the polar integral
Reason
The polar integral carries the Jacobian factor $\sin\varphi$, and that extra factor is what makes the integral elementary. The substitution $u = \cos\varphi$ turns $\sin\varphi\,d\varphi$ into $-du$, so the integral becomes a polynomial in $u$ over the interval $-1 \le u \le 1$. The result is a rational number that is slightly larger than one, and the excess over one is the exact excess of the body over the unit ball in the polar direction.
Computation
Set $u = \cos\varphi$, so that $du = -\sin\varphi\,d\varphi$ and $\varphi$ from $0$ to $\pi$ means $u$ from $1$ to $-1$. Then
$$ B = \int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{2}5\varphi\,\sin\varphi\,d\varphi = \int_{-1}^{1}\sin^{2}(5\arccos u)\,du . $$
Expand the square with $\sin^{2}v = \frac{1 - \cos 2v}{2}$ and use $2\arccos u$ as one angle:
$$ B = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}du - \frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\cos(10\arccos u)\,du . $$
The first integral is $1$. The second follows from the product-to-sum identity $\cos 10\varphi\,\sin\varphi = \frac{1}{2}\bigl(\sin 11\varphi - \sin 9\varphi\bigr)$, so
$$ \int_{0}^{\pi}\cos 10\varphi\,\sin\varphi\,d\varphi = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2}{11} - \frac{2}{9}\right) = -\frac{2}{99}, $$
This value follows because $\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin 11\varphi\,d\varphi = \frac{2}{11}$ and $\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin 9\varphi\,d\varphi = \frac{2}{9}$. The minus sign in front of the cosine term then gives
$$ B = 1 + \frac{1}{99} = \frac{100}{99}. $$
The same calculation for the harmonic $n$ gives
$$ B_{n} = 1 + \frac{1}{4n^{2} - 1}. $$
Result
The polar integral is $B = \frac{100}{99} = 1.0101010101$. The identity returns $\frac{36}{35}$ at $n = 3$, $\frac{100}{99}$ at $n = 5$, and $\frac{196}{195}$ at $n = 7$. The value is larger than one, so the polar direction adds a small amount of volume, and that amount is $\frac{1}{99}$ of the volume of the unit ball.
Common mistake
A reader may integrate $\sin^{2}5\varphi$ without the factor $\sin\varphi$ and obtain $\pi$, but the Jacobian factor belongs to the integrand and its presence is what reduces the integral to a rational number. A reader may also expect the polar integral to be one, because the average of a sine squared is one half, but the weight $\sin\varphi$ favours the equator, where $\sin^{2}5\varphi$ is large on most of the equator band, and the weighted average is slightly above one half of the maximum.

Part 3, Step 3: Assemble the volume in closed form
Reason
Each factor of the quadratic term now has an exact value, so the term itself has an exact value, and the volume is the constant term plus that value. Adding the two terms also shows the size of the effect in one number: the ratio of the volume of the body to the volume of the unit ball, which the statement implies because the surface is a perturbation of the sphere.
Computation
Insert $A = \pi$ and $B = \frac{100}{99}$ into the quadratic term of Part 2:
$$ T_{2} = \frac{AB}{25} = \frac{\pi}{25}\cdot\frac{100}{99} = \frac{4\pi}{99}. $$
Add the constant term:
$$ V = \frac{4\pi}{3} + \frac{4\pi}{99} = \frac{4\pi}{3}\left(1 + \frac{1}{33}\right) = \frac{4\pi}{3}\cdot\frac{34}{33} = \frac{136\pi}{99}. $$
The ratio to the unit ball is the bracket:
$$ \frac{V}{4\pi/3} = \frac{34}{33} = 1 + \frac{1}{33}. $$
Result
The volume is $V = \frac{136\pi}{99} = 4.31572324130$ cubic length units. The body exceeds the unit ball by the factor $\frac{34}{33}$, that is by $\frac{1}{33} = 0.0303030303$, which is about three percent. The excess itself is $\frac{4\pi}{99} = 0.126933036509$.
Common mistake
A reader may add the two terms as $\frac{4\pi}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{33}$ and forget the unit ball, but the constant term is part of the volume, so the sum carries both terms. A reader may also divide by $33$ twice, but the bracket $1 + \frac{1}{33}$ is already the ratio, and the ratio equals $\frac{34}{33}$.

Part 4. The independent numerical checks
Part 4, Step 1: Check the closed form by four numerical methods
Reason
A closed form that rests on one chain of algebra deserves an independent measurement, and the statement asks for a computer algebra system, which means the reader must be able to trust the algebra. Four methods with different failure modes give that trust: an adaptive quadrature of the two-angle integral, a midpoint sum that samples the integrand at a fixed grid, a random sampler whose error the script can estimate, and a volume count on a Cartesian grid that never uses spherical coordinates at all.
Computation
The four methods compute a value of $V$ and one error estimate each:
$$ \text{adaptive: } \left|\hat V - V\right| \le 10^{-10}, \qquad \text{midpoint: } \left|\hat V - V\right| = O(h^{2}), $$
$$ \text{random: } \left|\hat V - V\right| \le 3\,\sigma, \qquad \text{grid count: } \left|\hat V - V\right| = O(h), $$
In these expressions $\sigma$ is the standard error of the random estimate and $h$ is the step of the grid. The random method draws points in the cube $\left[-1.3, 1.3\right]^{3}$ and counts the share that falls inside the surface, so it uses the inequality $\rho \le$ the surface and no angle at all. The grid count tests the same inequality at the nodes of a Cartesian mesh and multiplies the share by the volume of the cube.
Result
The adaptive quadrature returns $4.3157232413$ against the exact $4.3157232413$, with a difference of $8.882 \times 10^{-16}$. The midpoint sum over $2000$ cells per angle returns $4.31572367194$, with a difference of $4.306 \times 10^{-7}$. The random sample of $8000000$ points returns $4.318276001$ with a standard error of $0.00267513$, a distance of $0.954$ standard errors from the exact value. The Cartesian count on $261$ points per axis returns $4.315427$, with a difference of $2.962 \times 10^{-4}$. Every method agrees with the closed form within its own error.
Common mistake
A reader may compare the random estimate with the exact value without its standard error and read the third decimal as an error of the algebra, but the difference lies inside one standard error, so the sample says nothing against the closed form. A reader may also expect the Cartesian count to converge as fast as the midpoint sum, but the count has an error of order $h$ against the order $h^{2}$ of the sum, because the count decides membership at a point and not on a cell.

Part 5. The peaks, the valleys, and the size of the excess
Part 5, Step 1: Count the peaks and the valleys
Reason
A single number describes the volume, but the reader also wants to know what the number measures. The surface has peaks where the radius is largest and valleys where it is smallest, and the count of those directions explains why the volume lands between the volume of the unit ball and the volume of the sphere of radius $\frac{6}{5}$. The count also tests the parameters $m = 6$ and $n = 5$ of the statement against the drawing of Figure 1.
Computation
The radius is largest where $\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\varphi = 1$. The sine of $5\varphi$ reaches $1$ at three values of $\varphi$ in the interval $0 \le \varphi \le \pi$, and the sine of $6\theta$ reaches $1$ at six values of $\theta$ in the interval $0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$, so the number of peaks is
$$ 3 \times 6 = 18 . $$
The radius is smallest where $\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\varphi = -1$. The sine of $5\varphi$ reaches $-1$ at two values of $\varphi$, because the value $\varphi = \pi$ is the pole and not a minimum of the sine, so the number of valleys is
$$ 2 \times 6 = 12 . $$
Six of the eighteen peaks lie on the equator $\varphi = \frac{\pi}{2}$, where the drawing of Figure 1 shows them as the bright band around the middle of the body.
Result
The script reports $18$ peak directions, three polar bands of peaks at $\varphi = 0.314159$, $1.570796$, and $2.827433$, two polar bands of valleys at $\varphi = 0.942478$ and $2.199115$, and six peaks on the equator. The marked peak sits at $\left(0.358185,\,0.095975,\,1.141268\right)$ with length $1.2$, and the marked valley sits at $\left(0.167511,\,0.625160,\,0.470228\right)$ with length $0.8$. The radius on the drawn map grid runs from $0.800122$ to $1.199878$, which are the two bounds of Step 1.1 seen through the resolution of the grid.
Common mistake
A reader may count the valleys as three bands per pole, but the sine of $5\varphi$ reaches $-1$ only twice in the interval $0 \le \varphi \le \pi$, so the count of valleys is $12$ and not $18$. A reader may also take the extremes of the drawn grid as exact bounds, but the grid samples the angles at a finite step, so its extremes fall short of $\frac{4}{5}$ and $\frac{6}{5}$ by $1.218 \times 10^{-4}$.

Part 6. The answer and the computer algebra session
Part 6, Step 1: State the answer and read it as a computer algebra session
Reason
The statement asks for a computer algebra system, so the page must show the session that the reader would type and the answer that the session returns. Writing the session also makes the structure of the solution visible: one definition, one integral, one closed form, and one comparison with the unit ball. The comparison is the part a reader remembers, because it says how much the bumps matter.
Computation
The session has four lines. The first line defines the radius. The second line integrates the volume element of Step 1.2 with the limits of Step 1.1. The third line simplifies the result. The fourth line divides by the volume of the unit ball.
$$ \rho = 1 + \frac{1}{5}\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\varphi, $$
$$ V = \int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{\rho^{3}}{3}\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta, $$
$$ V = \frac{136\pi}{99}, \qquad \frac{V}{4\pi/3} = \frac{34}{33} = 1 + \frac{1}{33}. $$
The two linear terms of the expansion vanish inside the session for the reason of Step 2.1, so the session returns a closed form in $\pi$ and not a special function.
