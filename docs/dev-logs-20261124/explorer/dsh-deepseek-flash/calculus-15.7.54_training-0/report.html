The average value of $f(x, y, z) = x^2 z + y^2 z$ over a paraboloid solid

A verified tutorial for exercise 54 of section 15.7. Every numerical claim is checked by a Python script, and each claim cites the file and the line range of its check.

Answer. $\displaystyle f_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{V(E)}\iiint_E \left(x^2 z + y^2 z\right) dV = \frac{\pi/24}{\pi/2} = \frac{1}{12} = 0.0833333$

The volume is $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, the triple integral is $\dfrac{\pi}{24}$, and the largest value of the integrand on the region is $\dfrac{1}{4}$.

The statement

The average value of a function $f(x, y, z)$ over a solid region $E$ is defined to be $\displaystyle f_{\mathrm{ave}} = \frac{1}{V(E)} \iiint_E f(x, y, z) \, dV,$ where $V(E)$ is the volume of $E$. For instance, if $\rho$ is a density function, then $\rho_{\mathrm{ave}}$ is the average density of $E$. Find the average value of the function $f(x, y, z) = x^2 z + y^2 z$ over the region enclosed by the paraboloid $z = 1 - x^2 - y^2$ and the plane $z = 0$.

Part 1, Step 1: name the body that the two surfaces enclose

Goal. Write the solid $E$ as a set of inequalities before any integral appears.

Reason. The statement says that the paraboloid $z = 1 - x^2 - y^2$ and the plane $z = 0$ enclose $E$, and it gives no inequality. The paraboloid opens downward, so its apex $(0, 0, 1)$ is its highest point. The two surfaces meet only where $1 - x^2 - y^2 = 0$, that is on the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ in the plane $z = 0$. A closed body needs a rim, so this circle is the rim of $E$.

Computation. Take a point with $x^2 + y^2 < 1$. There the height of the paraboloid is positive, so the paraboloid lies above the plane. The vertical line through that point meets the two surfaces at $z = 0$ and at $z = 1 - x^2 - y^2$, and it meets the body between them. Take a point with $x^2 + y^2 > 1$. There the paraboloid lies below the plane, and the two surfaces do not bound any point above the plane, so no point of $E$ lies there.

$$E = \left\{(x, y, z) : x^2 + y^2 \leq 1,\; 0 \leq z \leq 1 - x^2 - y^2\right\}$$

Result. The body is a cap of height $1$ over a base disk of radius $1$. Figure 1 shows the cap and its rim. The cap is closed: the paraboloid closes the top and the disk closes the bottom.

Verification. The printed check code/part1_verify.py:95-101 locates the rim twice. A bisection on $1 - r^2$ and the closed form $\sqrt{1}$ both give the radius $1.0$. The check code/part1_verify.py:149-161 reports the largest height $1.0$ by a grid search and by a bounded minimisation of $-z$.

Common mistake. A reader may take the body for the region above the paraboloid and below the plane $z = 2$, because the word "enclosed" suggests a box. The two surfaces meet at $z = 0$ alone, so the pair fixes one height, $0 \leq z \leq 1 - x^2 - y^2$, and no second ceiling.

Part 1, Step 2: project the body on the plane $z = 0$

Goal. Find the projection $D$ and its area, because every later integral uses $D$ as its outer region.

Reason. A projection onto the plane $z = 0$ drops the coordinate $z$ and keeps the coordinates $x$ and $y$. The projection of $E$ is therefore the set of pairs $(x, y)$ that occur in at least one point of $E$. Step 1 shows that a pair occurs in a point of $E$ exactly when $x^2 + y^2 \leq 1$, and the height range of that pair is not empty then.

Computation. Drop $z$ from the three inequalities of $E$. The inequalities $0 \leq z \leq 1 - x^2 - y^2$ hold for some $z$ exactly when $0 \leq 1 - x^2 - y^2$, that is exactly when $x^2 + y^2 \leq 1$.

$$D = \left\{(x, y) : x^2 + y^2 \leq 1\right\}, \qquad \operatorname{area}(D) = \pi \cdot 1^2 = \pi$$

Result. The projection is the closed unit disk in the plane $z = 0$, and its area is $\pi$. Figure 1 right panel draws it. The vertical line above each interior point of $D$ meets the body in one segment, so $D$ is also the region of the outer two integrals.

Verification. The check code/part1_verify.py:103-115 builds the boundary polygon from a root search and asks the polygon and the inequality about the same $231361$ plane points. The two answers disagree on $0$ points. The check code/part1_verify.py:117-130 measures the area of the polygon by the shoelace sum, $3.1415527794$, and the radial trapezoid sum, and both agree with $\pi$ inside $1.0 \times 10^{-4}$. The check code/part1_verify.py:163-173 counts the columns above $D$ and returns $1.5707932$ for the volume, which matches the value of Part 2 before Part 2 is written.

Common mistake. A reader may call this projection the projection on the plane $x = 0$, because the figure shows the disk in the plane of the page. The pair that survives is $(x, y)$, so the projection lies on the plane $z = 0$; a projection that kept $x$ and $z$ would lie on the plane $y = 0$ and would be a different region.

Part 1, Step 3: read the shape of each horizontal section

Goal. Decide the shape of the section at a fixed height $z$, because the volume and the integral both use that shape.

Reason. Fix a height $z$ between $0$ and $1$. A point of the section keeps that $z$ and must satisfy the two inequalities of Step 1 that involve $x$ and $y$. The second inequality, $z \leq 1 - x^2 - y^2$, becomes a disk condition, and the first inequality, $0 \leq z$, is already true for the fixed height.

Computation. For $0 \leq z \leq 1$ the section is $\{(x, y) : x^2 + y^2 \leq 1 - z\}$. This is a disk of radius $\sqrt{1 - z}$ and of area $\pi (1 - z)$. At $z = 1$ the disk shrinks to the single point $(0, 0)$. At a height above $1$ the section is empty. In the section at $y = 0$, the section at height $z$ appears as a chord from $x = -\sqrt{1 - z}$ to $x = \sqrt{1 - z}$, and Figure 2 draws three such chords.

Result. Each horizontal section is a disk, not an annulus, and its area is $\pi (1 - z)$. The area falls from $\pi$ at $z = 0$ to $0$ at $z = 1$, and the largest area is $\pi$.

Verification. The check code/part1_verify.py:132-147 reports the shape at five radii $r$ of the rim. The four values $r^2 = 0$, $0.25$, $0.75$, $1$ give the shape "segment" with the printed corner list $(0.0,\ 1 - r^2)$, the value $r^2 = 1.44$ gives "empty", and the value $r^2 = 1$ gives "point". The check code/part2_verify.py:83-97 compares the closed form $\pi (1 - z)$ with a grid count of the disk at $z = 0$, $0.5$, $0.8$.

Common mistake. A reader may take each horizontal section for an annulus, because the side wall of a bowl often reads as a ring. The inequalities give $x^2 + y^2 \leq 1 - z$, which is one closed disk around the axis; an annulus would need a lower bound of the form $x^2 + y^2 \geq a(z)$ with $a(z) > 0$, and no such bound occurs.

Part 2, Step 1: add the areas of the horizontal sections

Goal. Compute $V(E)$ by slicing the body at fixed heights.

Reason. Step 1.3 gives the area of every horizontal section as a function of the height alone, $\pi (1 - z)$. The slice method applies when the section at height $z$ has a known area for every height in the height range of the body, and here that range is $0 \leq z \leq 1$.

Computation. The volume is the integral of the section area over the height range.

$$V(E) = \int_{0}^{1} \pi (1 - z)\, dz = \pi \left[ z - \frac{z^2}{2} \right]_{0}^{1} = \pi \left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi}{2}$$

Figure 2 shows the section at $y = 0$ and the three chords whose lengths are the diameters of three sections. Each chord of length $2\sqrt{1 - z}$ gives the section area $\pi (1 - z)$.

Result. $V(E) = \dfrac{\pi}{2} = 1.5707963268$.

Verification. The check code/part2_verify.py:83-97 compares $\pi (1 - z)$ with a grid count at three heights. The check code/part2_verify.py:123-130 integrates the same area with the composite Simpson rule. The check code/part2_verify.py:99-121 integrates the constant $1$ over $E$ by two nested adaptive quadratures, once in Cartesian order and once in polar order, and both give $1.5707963268$ inside $1.0 \times 10^{-6}$.

Common mistake. A reader may add the section areas without the factor $dz$, which would add $1$ for every height and would return a number of the size of the height range. The slice formula is an integral of the area over the height, $V = \int A(z)\, dz$, and not a sum of areas.

Part 2, Step 2: repeat the volume in cylindrical coordinates

Goal. Obtain the same volume from a second order of integration, so that the answer does not depend on one order.

Reason. The body is a surface of revolution about the $z$ axis, and the two surfaces depend on $x^2 + y^2$ alone. Cylindrical coordinates therefore turn the disk $D$ into the rectangle $0 \leq r \leq 1$, $0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi$, and the height becomes $0 \leq z \leq 1 - r^2$.

Computation. With the volume element $r\, dz\, dr\, d\theta$ and the height range of Step 1.1,

$$V(E) = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1} \int_{0}^{1 - r^2} r\, dz\, dr\, d\theta = 2\pi \int_{0}^{1} r (1 - r^2)\, dr = 2\pi \left[ \frac{r^2}{2} - \frac{r^4}{4} \right]_{0}^{1} = 2\pi \cdot \frac{1}{4} = \frac{\pi}{2}$$

Result. Both orders give $V(E) = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$. The coefficient of $\pi$ in the radial route is $\dfrac{1}{4}$, and the factor $2\pi$ of the angle carries it to $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$.

Verification. The check code/part2_verify.py:70-81 computes the two coefficients with exact fractions. The radial route returns $\dfrac{1}{2}$, and the height route returns $\dfrac{1}{2}$. The check code/part2_verify.py:132-147 draws $400000$ points in the box $[-1, 1] \times [-1, 1] \times [0, 1]$ of volume $4$ and reports $1.57336$ with a standard error $0.0030895$, so the sample agrees with $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ inside one standard error.

Common mistake. A reader may drop the Jacobian factor $r$, because the limits already contain $r$. The limits $0 \leq r \leq 1$ are a length, and the area element of the disk is $r\, dr\, d\theta$; without the factor $r$ the same limits give $\dfrac{2\pi}{3}$, which is too large by the factor $\dfrac{4}{3}$.

Part 3, Step 1: write the integrand in cylindrical coordinates

Goal. Prepare the triple integral of $f(x, y, z) = x^2 z + y^2 z$ over $E$.

Reason. The function contains $x^2 + y^2$, and the body is a solid of revolution about the $z$ axis. Cylindrical coordinates therefore simplify both the function and the region at the same time, and the height range that Step 1.1 gives stays valid.

Computation. Factor the function, $f = (x^2 + y^2) z$, then substitute $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ and $dV = r\, dz\, dr\, d\theta$.

$$f(x, y, z)\, dV = r^2 z \cdot r\, dz\, dr\, d\theta = r^3 z\, dz\, dr\, d\theta$$

The region becomes $0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi$, $0 \leq r \leq 1$, $0 \leq z \leq 1 - r^2$.

Result. The triple integral that remains is

$$\iiint_{E} \left(x^2 z + y^2 z\right) dV = \int_{0}^{2\pi} \int_{0}^{1} \int_{0}^{1 - r^2} r^3 z\, dz\, dr\, d\theta$$

Verification. The check code/part3_verify.py:56-67 forms the coefficient of the $r$ integral with exact fractions and compares it with the coefficient of the horizontal route of Step 3.2; both return $\dfrac{1}{24}$ for the whole integral divided by $\pi$.

Common mistake. A reader may substitute $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ and keep the plane element $dx\, dy$, which drops the factor $r$ of the Jacobian. The volume element in cylindrical coordinates is $r\, dz\, dr\, d\theta$, so the integrand is $r^3 z$ and not $r^2 z$.

Part 3, Step 2: integrate the height first

Goal. Reduce the triple integral to one integral in $r$.

Reason. The height range depends on $r$ alone, $0 \leq z \leq 1 - r^2$, and the integrand is linear in $z$. The inner integral in $z$ is therefore elementary, and it removes $z$ from the problem without touching the limits in $r$ and $\theta$.

Computation. Integrate in $z$ first, then in $\theta$, then in $r$.

$$\int_{0}^{1 - r^2} r^3 z\, dz = r^3 \cdot \frac{(1 - r^2)^2}{2}$$

The angle contributes the factor $2\pi$, because no term contains $\theta$. The radial integral is the area under the curve of Figure 3, $g(r) = \dfrac{r^3 (1 - r^2)^2}{2}$, and it equals $\dfrac{1}{48}$:

$$\int_{0}^{1} \frac{r^3 (1 - r^2)^2}{2}\, dr = \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{1} \left(r^3 - 2 r^5 + r^7\right) dr = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{4} - \frac{2}{6} + \frac{1}{8}\right) = \frac{1}{48}$$

Therefore the triple integral is $2\pi \cdot \dfrac{1}{48} = \dfrac{\pi}{24}$.

Result. $\displaystyle\iiint_{E} \left(x^2 z + y^2 z\right) dV = \frac{\pi}{24} = 0.1308996939$. Figure 3 shows the radial integrand, its shaded area $\dfrac{1}{48}$, and its largest value $0.0458066708$ at $r^2 = \dfrac{3}{7}$.

Verification. The check code/part3_verify.py:69-79 integrates the same function slice by slice, with the moment of each disk, and returns the coefficient $\dfrac{1}{24}$. The check code/part3_verify.py:84-97 integrates over $E$ in Cartesian order and returns $0.1308996939$. The check code/part3_verify.py:112-131 reports $0.13077082$ with a standard error $0.00036251$. The check code/part3_verify.py:133-152 shows that the wrong integrands of the two common mistakes give $\dfrac{8\pi}{105}$ and $\dfrac{\pi}{6}$, which differ from $\dfrac{\pi}{24}$ by the factors $\dfrac{64}{35}$ and $4$.

Common mistake. A reader may take the inner integral in $z$ for the length $1 - r^2$ and drop the factor $z$ of the integrand. The integrand is $r^3 z$, so the inner integral gives $r^3 \dfrac{(1 - r^2)^2}{2}$; the length alone would give the wrong mass $\dfrac{11}{24} + \dfrac{3\pi}{64}$ in place of the correct value.

Part 4, Step 1: divide the integral by the volume

Goal. Apply the definition of the average value.

Reason. The statement defines the average value of $f$ over $E$ as the triple integral of $f$ divided by the volume of $E$. Part 2 gives the volume and Part 3 gives the integral, so the average is a ratio of two checked numbers.

Computation.

$$f_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{V(E)} \iiint_{E} f\, dV = \frac{\pi / 24}{\pi / 2} = \frac{\pi}{24} \cdot \frac{2}{\pi} = \frac{2}{24} = \frac{1}{12}$$

The factor $\pi$ cancels, so the answer does not contain $\pi$.

Result. $f_{\text{ave}} = \dfrac{1}{12} = 0.0833333$ to seven decimal places.

Verification. The check code/part4_verify.py:50-61 divides the exact fraction of the integral by the exact fraction of the volume and returns $\dfrac{1}{12}$. The check code/part4_verify.py:63-73 divides two adaptive quadratures and returns $0.0833333333$. The check code/part4_verify.py:127-135 rounds the exact fraction to seven decimal places and returns the string $0.0833333$.

Common mistake. A reader may stop at the triple integral, because the phrase "average value" reads like a property of the function alone. The definition divides by the volume of the region; without the division the result is $0.1308997$, which is the integral and not the average.

Part 4, Step 2: bound the answer by the extreme values of the function

Goal. Check the answer against the range of the function on $E$.

Reason. The average of a function over a region lies between the least and the greatest value of the function on that region. This bound is a test of the arithmetic of Step 4.1 that uses no integral, so it can fail only when the division is wrong.

Computation. On $E$ the function is $f = r^2 z$ with $r^2 \leq 1$ and $0 \leq z \leq 1 - r^2$. Write $s = r^2$ and $z = (1 - s) u$ with $0 \leq u \leq 1$. Then $f = s (1 - s) u$, so $f \leq s (1 - s) \leq \dfrac{1}{4}$, and the bound $\dfrac{1}{4}$ is attained at $s = \dfrac{1}{2}$, $u = 1$, that is at $r^2 = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and $z = \dfrac{1}{2}$. The least value is $0$, at every point of the axis and at the rim.

$$0 \leq f_{\text{ave}} = \frac{1}{12} \leq \frac{1}{4}$$

The average is one third of the greatest value.

Result. The answer $\dfrac{1}{12}$ lies inside the range $\left[0, \dfrac{1}{4}\right]$, and it is exactly $\dfrac{1}{3}$ of the greatest value $\dfrac{1}{4}$ at the point $\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}, 0, \dfrac{1}{2}\right)$.

Verification. The check code/part4_verify.py:75-104 maximises $f$ on $E$ with a bounded quasi-Newton search over the two parameters $s$ and $u$ and returns $0.2499999999993946$ against $\dfrac{1}{4}$. The check code/part4_verify.py:106-114 compares the average with both extremes. The check code/part4_verify.py:116-125 shows that the answer of a reader who drops the division is above the greatest value, and that the answer of a reader who divides by the base area $\pi$ is $0.0416667$.

Common mistake. A reader may take the greatest value of $f$ for $1$, because both $x^2 + z$ and $y^2 + z$ reach $1$ at a corner of a bounding box. The region couples the two variables through $z \leq 1 - r^2$, so the largest value is $\dfrac{1}{4}$ at $r^2 = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and not $1$; a reader who weights only the density $x^2 + y^2$ gets $\dfrac{17}{60} + \dfrac{\pi}{16}$ in place of $\dfrac{11}{24} + \dfrac{3\pi}{32}$.

Part 5, Step 1: check the whole chain with two independent routes

Goal. Confirm the five numbers of the tutorial by routes that share no step with Parts 2 to 4.

Reason. A chain of exact fractions can carry one arithmetic slip from step to step and still look consistent, because every later step repeats the same slip. An independent route is a route that starts from the region only: it uses no antiderivative of the earlier parts, and it uses a sample whose error the script can estimate.

Computation. The first route expands the polynomial integrand in the single variable $r$ and integrates term by term with a library that stores the coefficients, so the powers and the coefficients come from two different places. The second route draws $800000$ random points in the box $[-1, 1] \times [-1, 1] \times [0, 1]$, keeps the points that lie in $E$, and averages $f$ over the kept points.

$$V(E) \approx 1.57458, \qquad \iiint_{E} f\, dV \approx 0.13102786, \qquad f_{\text{ave}} \approx 0.08321448$$

The standard error of the average is $0.00011486$, so the sample agrees with $\dfrac{1}{12}$ inside one standard error.

Result. Both routes return $V(E) = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, $\iiint_{E} f\, dV = \dfrac{\pi}{24}$, and $f_{\text{ave}} = \dfrac{1}{12}$. The tutorial answer is therefore stable under a change of method.

Verification. The check code/part5_verify.py:37-50 returns $1.5707963268$, $0.1308996939$, and $0.0833333333$. The check code/part5_verify.py:52-68 returns the sample average with its standard error. The check code/part5_verify.py:70-79 returns the volume and the integral of the same sample. The check code/part5_verify.py:81-92 confirms that the exact ratio of the two exact fractions is $\dfrac{1}{12}$ once more, by a comparison of two fractions that the script builds separately.

Common mistake. A reader may treat a sample that agrees inside two standard errors as a proof of the exact value. A sample only shows that the exact value is consistent with the data; the proof of $\dfrac{1}{12}$ rests on the exact fractions of Parts 2 to 4, and the sample is a guard against a large slip.
