Stewart, Calculus: Early Transcendentals · Section 15.9 · Exercise 45

Volume Inside a Bumpy Sphere

Problem

The surfaces \(\rho = 1 + \frac{1}{5}\sin m\theta\,\sin n\phi\) have been used as models for tumors. The "bumpy sphere" with \(m = 6\) and \(n = 5\) is shown. Use a computer algebra system to find the volume it encloses.

Reading the shape

The surface is written in spherical coordinates. The symbol \(\rho\) is the distance from the origin, \(\theta\) is the angle around the \(z\)-axis, and \(\phi\) is the angle from the positive \(z\)-axis. With \(m = 6\) and \(n = 5\) the surface is

\[ \rho = 1 + \frac{1}{5}\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\phi. \]

The factor \(\frac{1}{5}\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\phi\) is the bump. It never exceeds \(\frac{1}{5}\) in absolute value, so the radius stays between \(0.8\) and \(1.2\) and the solid is star-shaped about the origin. The six bumps around the equator come from \(\sin 6\theta\), and the five rings of bumps from the pole to the equator come from \(\sin 5\phi\).

A meridian shows the bump in one plane. Along the meridian \(\theta = \pi/12\) the factor \(\sin 6\theta\) equals \(1\), so that meridian reaches \(\rho = 1.2\). Along \(\theta = \pi/4\) it equals \(-1\), so that meridian shrinks to \(\rho = 0.8\).

Setting up the integral

In spherical coordinates the volume element is \(dV = \rho^{2}\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\). The solid is the set of points with \(0 \le \rho \le 1 + \frac{1}{5}\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\phi\), \(0 \le \phi \le \pi\), and \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\). The radial integral is immediate:

\[ V = \int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{\rho(\theta,\phi)} \rho^{2}\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta = \frac{1}{3}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \left(1 + \frac{1}{5}\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\phi\right)^{3}\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta. \]

Expanding the cube

Write \(u = \frac{1}{5}\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\phi\). Then

\[ (1 + u)^{3} = 1 + 3u + 3u^{2} + u^{3}. \]

Each term is integrated against \(\sin\phi\) over the sphere of directions. Three of the four terms vanish, and each one vanishes for a reason.

The linear term. It contains \(\sin 6\theta\), and

\[ \int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin 6\theta\,d\theta = 0, \qquad \int_{0}^{\pi}\sin 5\phi\,\sin\phi\,d\phi = 0. \]

The second integral vanishes because \(\sin 5\phi\) is odd about \(\phi = \pi/2\) while \(\sin\phi\) is even there.

The cubic term. It contains \(\sin^{3}6\theta\), and

\[ \int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin^{3}6\theta\,d\theta = 0, \qquad \int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{3}5\phi\,\sin\phi\,d\phi = 0. \]

The square term. This term survives. The square removes the sign, because \(\sin^{2}\) has period \(\pi\) while \(\sin\) has period \(2\pi\):

\[ \int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin^{2}6\theta\,d\theta = \pi, \qquad \int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{2}5\phi\,\sin\phi\,d\phi = \frac{100}{99}. \]

The second value follows from \(\sin^{2}5\phi = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 - \cos 10\phi\right)\):

\[ \int_{0}^{\pi}\sin^{2}5\phi\,\sin\phi\,d\phi = \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin\phi\,d\phi - \frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos 10\phi\,\sin\phi\,d\phi = 1 - \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{99} = \frac{100}{99}, \]

where \(\cos 10\phi\,\sin\phi = \frac{1}{2}\left(\sin 11\phi - \sin 9\phi\right)\) and \(\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin k\phi\,d\phi = \frac{2}{k}\) for odd \(k\).

Adding the surviving terms

The constant term.

\[ \int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta = 2\pi \cdot 2 = 4\pi. \]

The square term.

\[ 3\left(\frac{1}{5}\right)^{2}\pi\cdot\frac{100}{99} = \frac{3\pi}{25}\cdot\frac{100}{99} = \frac{4\pi}{33}. \]

Add the two results and divide by \(3\):

\[ V = \frac{1}{3}\left(4\pi + \frac{4\pi}{33}\right) = \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{136\pi}{33} = \frac{136\pi}{99} \approx 4.3157. \]

Checking the value

The unit sphere holds \(4\pi/3 \approx 4.1888\). The bumpy sphere must be slightly larger, because the mean of \(\rho\) over all directions is \(1\) while the mean of \(\rho^{3}\) is larger than \(1\). The computed ratio confirms this:

\[ \frac{V}{4\pi/3} = \frac{34}{33} \approx 1.0303, \qquad \frac{3V}{4\pi} = \frac{34}{33} \approx 1.0303. \]

Three independent computations give the same value \(4.315723\). They are the term-by-term integrals above, a double quadrature of the exact integral, and a Monte Carlo average of \(\rho^{3}\) over uniformly distributed directions. The Monte Carlo value is \(4.315040\), which agrees to four digits.

Answer

The surface encloses the volume

\[ V = \frac{1}{3}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi} \left(1 + \frac{1}{5}\sin 6\theta\,\sin 5\phi\right)^{3}\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta = \frac{136\pi}{99} \approx 4.3157. \]

Only the constant term and the square term of the expanded cube contribute. The linear term and the cubic term give zero.

Notation

SymbolMeaning
\(\rho\)The distance from the origin in spherical coordinates, \(\rho \ge 0\). On this surface \(\rho\) is a function of the two angles.
\(\theta\)The angle around the \(z\)-axis, measured from the positive \(x\)-axis, \(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\).
\(\phi\)The polar angle, measured from the positive \(z\)-axis, \(0 \le \phi \le \pi\).
\(dV = \rho^{2}\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\)The volume element in spherical coordinates. The factor \(\rho^{2}\sin\phi\) is the Jacobian.
\(m = 6\), \(n = 5\)The numbers of bumps around the equator and from pole to pole. The product \(\sin m\theta\,\sin n\phi\) makes the pattern repeat.
star-shapedA solid is star-shaped about the origin when every ray from the origin meets its boundary once. Here that holds because \(\rho(\theta,\phi) > 0\) for every pair of angles.
mean over directionsThe average of a quantity over all directions, with weight \(\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta\) and total \(4\pi\).
