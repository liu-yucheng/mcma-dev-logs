Literature Review — Chapter 1

The Evolving Landscape of Multimodal Mathematical Reasoning Evaluation

📊 To understand the research gaps that relate to a math-tutoring agent, the review first sets the baseline for evaluating multimodal mathematical reasoning. The community has moved past text-only benchmarks such as MATH. It now uses more complex, visually grounded assessments. These benchmarks define the state of the field. Their limits reveal the first set of research gaps.

🧱 1.1 Foundational and holistic benchmarks

The first wave of multimodal math benchmarks aimed to create broad testbeds for the general mathematical reasoning of VLMs and MLLMs.

MathVista

Introduced in 2023, MathVista (Lu et al., 2023; arXiv:2310.02255) [1] is a landmark effort to build a holistic benchmark for multimodal mathematical reasoning. It contains 6,141 examples from 31 distinct datasets. It is one of the first large-scale datasets for this purpose [2][3]. Its official project page is https://mathvista.github.io/ [4][5]. However, studies that use MathVista show that even top models struggle with its problems. This is especially true for problems with “complex figures” and problems that need “rigorous reasoning” [6]. This shows a foundational gap: we can test for this reasoning, but current models lack the skill.

MMMU — Massive Multi-discipline Multimodal Understanding

This benchmark expands evaluation to college level across 30 subjects, including Science and Engineering. It requires expert-level knowledge [7]. It tests for “advanced perception and reasoning” [8]. It may contain calculus problems. But its broad scope means it is not built to test the fine-grained skills needed for generative tasks such as surface sketching. Its existence shows the community's ambition to handle undergraduate and expert-level material. That matches the target domain of a calculus-tutoring agent.

🔬 1.2 Second-generation benchmarks targeting deeper understanding

More recent benchmarks address specific weaknesses in earlier evaluations. They focus on whether models truly understand the visual parts of a problem. They also test whether models rely on spurious correlations instead.

MathVerse

Published in 2024 (Zhang et al.; arXiv:2403.14624), MathVerse is built to test “whether Multi-modal LLMs (MLLMs) truly understand diagrams in visual math problems” [9][10]. Its official project page is https://mathverse-cuhk.github.io [11][12]. This benchmark contains 2,612 problems. Crucially, it introduces multiple variants for each problem. This supports step-wise Chain-of-Thought (CoT) evaluation [13]. The focus on “truly seeing” the diagrams answers the finding that many MLLMs fail on earlier benchmarks due to shallow visual processing [14].

MATH-Vision

Also from 2024 (Wang et al.; arXiv:2402.14804), this benchmark focuses on high-quality problems from real math competitions [15][16][17]. Its paper is titled “Measuring Multimodal Mathematical Reasoning with the MATH-Vision Dataset.” Its project page is https://mathvision-cuhk.github.io [18]. Like MathVerse, it pushes for higher-standard visual and logical integration.

Specialized niche benchmarks

Other recent benchmarks target even more specific reasoning facets. They include:

  • MV-MATH (Wang et al., 2025; arXiv:2502.20808). It focuses on problems that need reasoning across multiple images or visual contexts. This skill is not well covered by single-image benchmarks [19][20][21].
  • WE-MATH (Qiao et al., 2024; arXiv:2407.01284). It is built for “fine-grained mathematical-knowledge evaluation.” It aims to test whether a model has human-like mathematical reasoning skills [22][23].
  • VisioMath and GeoQA. They target figure-based mathematical reasoning and geometric question-answering. They represent earlier but important steps in this domain [24].

🕳️ 1.3 Research gap: the absence of generative and agentic benchmarking

A critical look at these benchmarks reveals a large research gap directly relevant to a math-tutoring agent. Current benchmarks are mostly discriminative or single-turn generative. They present a static problem (text + image). They then grade the final answer or the textual reasoning path (CoT).

What they fail to evaluate

🧰
Tool use and manipulation

No existing math benchmark tests an agent's ability to select, set up, and run external tools such as pyplot or PGFplots. The evaluation stops at the model's textual or multiple-choice output.

🔁
Iterative revision and self-correction

Generating a plot, checking it, and revising it is a complex, multi-step agentic process. Benchmarks such as MathVerse test step-wise reasoning. They do not test step-wise action and correction in a generative task.

🎨
Generative fidelity and correctness

There is no framework for judging the quality and correctness of a generated mathematical visualization, such as a calculus surface sketch. Is the surface topologically correct? Are the axes labelled well? Does it show the given function exactly? These are detailed evaluation criteria. They go far beyond “is the final answer correct?”

🎓
Pedagogical utility

In a tutoring context, a “correct” plot may not be a “good” plot. A good plot might highlight specific features (for example, critical points, inflection points) to help student understanding. No benchmark tests the pedagogical value of a generated artifact.

The first open problem

❓ How do we design a benchmark to evaluate the full lifecycle of an autonomous, multimodal math tutoring agent—from problem understanding, to tool-based generative action, to iterative revision, to pedagogical effectiveness?

The capabilities of such an agent require a new benchmark. Current standards cannot measure its core skills. General agent benchmarks such as AgentVista [25] and OSWorld [26] are emerging for GUI and web tasks. But a specialized counterpart for scientific and educational generative tasks is missing.
